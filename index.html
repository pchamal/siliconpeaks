Four companies, public facts

What already happened, with the articles attached.

01 — Marketplace

Daraz

Rocket Internet built it. Alibaba bought the whole thing. Nepal was one of the five markets in the deal.

Daraz Group was founded in 2012 by Rocket Internet, first in Pakistan, then as a marketplace across Bangladesh, Myanmar, Sri Lanka, and Nepal. On 8 May 2018, Rocket Internet said Alibaba Group had acquired the entire share capital of Daraz. Contemporaneous reports — TechCrunch, Reuters, Rocket’s own notice — left the price undisclosed. Alibaba’s later SEC reporting recorded cash consideration of $194 million for 100% of the equity.

The five markets had a combined population of more than 460 million. Daraz kept its brand. For Kathmandu that meant a consumer-internet company serving Nepal sat inside a NYSE-listed group, with the logistics and payments stack that comes with that ownership. It is not a Kathmandu founding story. It is a control story: a South Asian marketplace with a Nepal operation became, in a single close, an Alibaba subsidiary.

If the question is whether global platforms treat this market as real enough to own, the 2018 purchase is the public answer. The operating company is still daraz.com.np.

02 — Listing

Fusemachines

Nasdaq: FUSE. The floor is in New York. Most of the people are in Nepal.

Fusemachines Inc. began trading on the Nasdaq Global Market on 23 October 2025 under the ticker FUSE, after a business combination with CSLM Acquisition Corp. The company, founded in 2013 by Sameer Maskey, sells enterprise AI products and services. The listing documents and the company’s own release are the primary record; ICT Frame, reporting from Kathmandu the next day, put about 260 of roughly 350 staff in Nepal.

That split is the fact that matters here. A Delaware issuer can list in the United States and still run the majority of its technical workforce from Kathmandu. It is the first company with that operational shape to reach a major American exchange. It is not a NEPSE listing, and it does not, by itself, create a domestic public-market path for Nepali equity. It does put a Nepal-built AI operation on a tape a US allocator already watches.

Fusemachines remains on this site’s public-company list for that reason, and not because a hotel or a bottler has a franchise down the road.

03 — Workforce

CloudFactory

$65 million in 2019, led by FTV Capital. The Series B was earlier, and smaller.

CloudFactory, founded by Mark Sears, sells managed teams for data work — the human layer behind machine-learning pipelines — and has long run a large operation in Nepal. On 24 May 2017 the company announced a $7.3 million Series B, with Dolma Impact Fund and The Social Entrepreneurs’ Fund among the backers. That is the Series B in the primary record.

On 20 November 2019 CloudFactory announced $65 million in growth equity led by FTV Capital, with Weatherford Capital participating. FTV’s own notice and the company blog agree on the figure, the date, and the lead. The money was earmarked for technology, workforce, and market expansion — a scaling round on top of a Nepal-centered labor model, not a seed myth.

Taken together, the two public rounds are how a Kathmandu workforce company entered the book of a US growth-equity firm. The 2019 check is the one people remember. The 2017 filing is the one that is actually called Series B.

CloudFactory, 20 Nov 2019 FTV Capital, 2019 CloudFactory Series B, 24 May 2017
04 — Floor

SecurityPal

A Security Operations Command Center in Kathmandu, opened with the US ambassador in the room.

SecurityPal, founded by Pukar Hamal, completes enterprise security reviews and questionnaires. On 20 September 2022 it emerged from stealth with a $21 million Series A led by Craft Ventures, with Martin Casado of Andreessen Horowitz participating — the company’s release, later echoed by Business Insider and by Forbes on 29 January 2025.

On 21 February 2023 the company inaugurated a 24/7 Security Operations Command Center in Kathmandu with Dean R. Thompson, then the United States ambassador to Nepal. PR Newswire and the Kathmandu Post covered the opening the same day. Nepali Times, writing on 24 August 2026, put more than 210 Nepali IT professionals in that operation.

The SOCC is the part that is hard to wave away from a distance: a named facility, a sitting ambassador, a headcount in the hundreds, and a book of US enterprise customers. Silicon Peaks, as a phrase, is what Hamal has used for that floor and for the wider bench around it. This site keeps the name in the public domain so the floor can be cited without asking anyone’s permission.

Diaspora

The bench is not only in Kathmandu.

More than 4.5 million people of Nepali origin live outside the country, with Non-Resident Nepali Association councils in 90 countries. In FY 2024/25 they sent home $12.64 billion — Nepal Rastra Bank’s dollar figure — on the order of 28% of GDP. About 800,000 are permanent expatriates in developed economies. That is the published shape of the network: Gulf labor, North American and European professionals, and a remittance line that dwarfs the IT account.

The export number and the remittance number are different facts. One is software. The other is the reason a diligence memo from Kathmandu should assume a global bench, not a single city.
